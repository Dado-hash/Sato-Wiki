## base

A store of value is any asset that preserves its purchasing power over long periods of time. The defining characteristic is simple: if you acquire it today, you expect it to hold roughly the same real value — or more — when you sell it years or decades later.

Throughout history, societies have chosen different stores of value. Gold has been the most enduring, serving as a reliable store for thousands of years. Real estate and fine art also function as stores of value, though they require maintenance, carry high transaction costs, and are illiquid. More recently, fiat currencies (government-issued money like the US dollar or euro) have been used as stores of value, but their purchasing power tends to erode over time due to inflation.

![Store of value comparison](media/wiki/store-of-value/store-of-value-hero.svg "Comparison of store of value properties across Bitcoin, Gold, Fiat, and Real Estate across six dimensions: scarcity, durability, portability, divisibility, verifiability, and history of monetary premium.")

Bitcoin introduces a new category: a purely digital store of value with properties that rival or exceed traditional assets. It has a fixed supply of 21 million coins, making it absolutely scarce. It is durable because the network continues operating as long as there is at least one node running. It is highly portable — you can send millions of dollars worth of bitcoin across the world in minutes with minimal fees. And it is verifiable: anyone can audit the entire supply and transaction history.

A useful metaphor for thinking about store of value is the **taxi comparison**. Imagine money as a taxi you take for part of your journey through life. Each time you enter the taxi, you want it to still be worth something when you get out. Fiat is like a taxi with a leaky meter — the value drips away as you ride. Gold is a sturdy taxi but hard to park and move. Bitcoin is a digital taxi with a locked meter: you know exactly what you have, and no one can change the fare.

## medium

To understand what makes a good store of value, we need to examine the properties that an asset must possess. Six properties are particularly important:

### Scarcity

A good store of value must have a supply that is limited or grows at a slow, predictable rate. If new units can be created freely, the existing units lose value through dilution.

- **Bitcoin (10/10):** Absolutely scarce. The 21 million cap is enforced by code, not by promise. No human decision can increase it.
- **Gold (7/10):** Geologically scarce but supply grows 1-2% annually as mining technology improves and new deposits are found.
- **Fiat (1/10):** Supply is entirely discretionary. Central banks can — and do — create unlimited amounts.
- **Real estate (6/10):** Land is physically scarce in prime locations, but new construction and zoning changes can increase usable supply.

### Durability

The asset must not degrade, rot, corrode, or disappear over time.

- **Bitcoin (9/10):** The blockchain persists as long as even one node exists. Data can be replicated infinitely. The only risk is a total loss of the global internet.
- **Gold (10/10):** Virtually indestructible. Gold does not tarnish, rust, or decay. Gold artifacts thousands of years old remain pristine.
- **Fiat (3/10):** Physical notes wear out, but the real problem is that value erodes through inflation, not physical degradation.
- **Real estate (8/10):** Buildings require ongoing maintenance and can be destroyed by natural disasters. Land itself is permanently durable.

### Portability

The asset must be easy to transport, transfer, and exchange across distances.

- **Bitcoin (10/10):** A private key weighing a few grams can control any amount of value. Transactions clear globally in under an hour (on-chain) or seconds (Lightning Network).
- **Gold (2/10):** Heavy and expensive to move and secure. Transporting large amounts requires armored vehicles and insurance.
- **Fiat (9/10):** Digital fiat moves easily through banking systems, but is subject to capital controls, freezes, and settlement delays.
- **Real estate (1/10):** Immovable by nature. Transfer requires lawyers, notaries, registries, and weeks of paperwork.

### Divisibility

The asset must be dividable into small units to facilitate exchange of any size.

- **Bitcoin (10/10):** Divisible to 8 decimal places (100 million satoshis per bitcoin). Future protocol upgrades could increase divisibility further.
- **Gold (1/10):** Malleable and meltable, but impractical to divide into tiny units for everyday exchange. Requires assay for purity verification.
- **Fiat (9/10):** Divisible to two decimal places for most currencies, with digital systems handling smaller fractions.
- **Real estate (2/10):** Extremely difficult to divide. Selling a fraction of a property requires legal contracts and shared ownership structures.

### Verifiability

The holder must be able to confirm the asset is genuine without relying on a third party.

- **Bitcoin (10/10):** Anyone can run a full node and independently verify the entire supply, transaction history, and ownership rules.
- **Gold (4/10):** Requires an assayer or specialized equipment (density test, XRF scanner) to verify purity. Counterfeiting is possible with tungsten-filled bars.
- **Fiat (5/10):** Basic verification by feel and sight; advanced counterfeits can fool experts. Digital balances rely entirely on bank statements.
- **Real estate (6/10):** Title can be verified through public registries, but the process varies by jurisdiction and fraud does occur.

### History of Monetary Premium

An asset with a long track record of being valued as money carries a "monetary premium" — extra value derived from its historical role as a medium of exchange and store of value.

- **Bitcoin (2/10):** Only 17 years old. The monetary premium is still being built and is not yet proven across generations.
- **Gold (10/10):** Over 5,000 years of continuous monetary use. Gold's premium is baked into human psychology and culture.
- **Fiat (7/10):** Modern fiat has operated globally since the 1971 Nixon Shock. Some currencies have centuries of history, though none as a reliable store of value.
- **Real estate (8/10):** Land has been a store of value since agriculture began. Its monetary premium is linked to civilization itself.

### Stock-to-Flow Model

The stock-to-flow (SF) ratio measures scarcity by comparing the existing stock of an asset to its annual new production:

```
SF = existing_supply / annual_new_production
```

- Gold SF: ~55 (it would take 55 years of mining to double the supply)
- Bitcoin SF (2026): ~110 (post-halving, it would take 110 years at current issuance)
- Fiat SF: varies wildly; the dollar's M2 money supply grew over 40% from 2020 to 2022 alone

Bitcoin's SF ratio doubles every four years with each halving. Proponents of the stock-to-flow model argue that this predictable increase in scarcity drives Bitcoin's long-term price appreciation. Critics note that the model is purely empirical and does not account for demand shocks or competing assets.

### Monetary Premium

The monetary premium is the extra value an asset carries beyond its practical utility, purely because people believe others will accept it as money.

Gold's monetary premium, for example, is estimated to be 50-80% of its total market capitalization. Gold's industrial uses (electronics, dentistry, jewelry) alone would not justify its $15+ trillion market cap. The premium comes from millennia of monetary tradition.

Bitcoin's monetary premium is even more extreme: its practical utility as a payment network is real but modest compared to its $1+ trillion market cap. The majority of Bitcoin's value derives from its perceived future role as a global store of value. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: the more people accept it as a store of value, the more valuable it becomes, which attracts more holders.

## advanced

### Network Effects of Store of Value Adoption

Store of value status is not a property that an asset simply possesses — it emerges from a network effect. The value of a store increases as more people adopt it, which in turn attracts more adopters. This is known as a **monetary network effect**.

For Bitcoin, this network effect operates across several dimensions:
- **Liquidity:** More holders mean deeper markets, lower spreads, and less slippage for large trades
- **Merchant acceptance:** More users encourage merchants to accept Bitcoin, which makes it more useful
- **Infrastructure:** More adoption drives investment in custody, trading, and payment infrastructure
- **Regulatory clarity:** Widespread use forces regulators to create clear frameworks
- **Psychological entrenchment:** Each new cohort of holders reinforces Bitcoin's status in the collective mind

This creates a powerful moat. Once an asset becomes established as a store of value, it is very difficult to displace — as gold's 5,000-year reign demonstrates.

### The Lindy Effect

The Lindy effect states that the future life expectancy of a non-perishable thing is proportional to its current age. For stores of value, this means that the longer something has been used as money, the longer it is likely to continue being used as money.

Gold's 5,000-year track record makes it extremely Lindy. Bitcoin, at only 17 years, is at the opposite end — it is still in what Lindy would call its "fragile" period. Each year that Bitcoin survives, its expected remaining lifespan increases. This is why the 21 million cap is so important: if Bitcoin were to abandon fixed supply, it would reset this clock and destroy the credibility built over nearly two decades.

### Gresham's Law in Reverse

Classical Gresham's law states that "bad money drives out good": when two forms of money circulate, people hoard the good money and spend the bad. Under a gold standard, people would hoard gold coins and spend debased silver or paper.

In the modern context, Gresham's law operates in reverse for Bitcoin. As Bitcoin is recognized as "good money" (hard, scarce, non-inflatable), it is hoarded rather than spent. People prefer to spend inflating fiat currency and save in Bitcoin. This reverse Gresham dynamic accelerates Bitcoin's transition from medium of exchange to store of value: the better Bitcoin proves itself as money, the less it circulates as a medium of exchange.

### The Theory of Monetization

Monetization is the process by which a commodity transitions into a monetary asset. The historical sequence typically follows:

1. **Collectible:** The object is valued for its aesthetic or novelty appeal
2. **Store of value:** People begin hoarding it because it holds value over time
3. **Medium of exchange:** The object is used in transactions because it is widely valued
4. **Unit of account:** Prices are denominated in the object because it has become the standard

Bitcoin is currently transitioning from stage 1/2 toward stage 3. It is already a well-established store of value for millions of people. Its use as a medium of exchange is growing through the Lightning Network, but it is not yet a unit of account in any major economy.

### Critiques of Bitcoin as a Store of Value

**Volatility.** The most common critique: if Bitcoin can drop 30% in a month, how can it be a store of value? The response is that store of value must be evaluated over decades, not weeks. Fiat currencies lose 2-3% annually with mild compounding; Bitcoin's volatility is high in the short term but its trend over multi-year periods has been strongly upward. As market capitalization grows and adoption matures, volatility is expected to decrease — a pattern seen in all maturing asset classes.

**Regulatory risk.** Governments could ban or severely restrict Bitcoin, limiting its ability to function as a store of value. While a total global ban is unlikely due to Bitcoin's decentralized nature and the difficulty of enforcement, significant regulatory headwinds in major economies could suppress adoption. The counterargument is that regulatory clarity tends to increase institutional adoption, and Bitcoin has survived a decade of regulatory attacks.

**Competition from other cryptocurrencies.** Thousands of other digital assets compete for the store of value narrative. Most have worse monetary policies (higher inflation, no cap, premines) or weaker security models. Ethereum, for example, has no supply cap and uses proof of stake. The primary competitor is gold-backed tokens or central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), which trade decentralization for stability. Bitcoin's first-mover advantage, network effects, and proof-of-work security give it a substantial lead.

### The Cantillon Effect

The Cantillon effect describes how newly created money enters the economy unevenly, benefiting those closest to the money creation source at the expense of those farthest away. When a central bank creates new money, it first enters the financial sector — banks and large institutions — who spend it before prices have adjusted upward. By the time the money reaches ordinary savers through wages and price increases, its purchasing power has already been diluted.

This makes fiat currency a systematically unfair store of value. Those with access to cheap credit (the wealthy, well-connected, and financial institutions) can acquire assets before inflation reduces their purchasing power, while savers without such access see their cash holdings steadily erode.

Bitcoin's fixed supply eliminates the Cantillon effect entirely. No one can create new bitcoin ahead of anyone else. Every satoshi is created according to a transparent, predictable schedule. The only distribution advantage is the one Satoshi himself held by mining early blocks — but this is a one-time historical event, not an ongoing structural advantage.

Bitcoin's issuance schedule could be described as a **reverse Cantillon system**: the earliest adopters bore the most risk and uncertainty, and later adopters pay a premium for that reduced uncertainty. This is the opposite of fiat, where later holders bear the cost of inflation created for early recipients of new money.

### Collateralized Commodity vs Monetary Premium

There is an ongoing debate about what kind of money Bitcoin represents:

- **The collateralized commodity view:** Bitcoin's value is anchored by the energy cost of mining (proof of work). Like gold, it costs real resources to produce, which provides a floor on its value. Under this view, Bitcoin is a digital commodity that is *mined* rather than *issued*.
- **The monetary premium view:** Bitcoin's value is almost entirely monetary premium — belief in its future role as global money. The energy cost of mining is an equilibrium condition, not a source of value. Under this view, if belief collapses, Bitcoin's value goes to zero regardless of mining cost.

Both views contain partial truths. The cost of production sets a long-term floor (miners will not sell below cost for extended periods), but the market price is determined by the intersection of supply and demand, which is driven by monetary premium. The two are not in conflict: gold's price is also far above its marginal extraction cost because of its monetary premium.

The key insight is that Bitcoin's monetary premium is self-consistent: it is valuable because people believe it will be valuable, and that belief is reinforced by the objective properties (fixed supply, verifiability, portability) that make it an ideal store of value. This is not a circular argument — it is the same mechanism that makes all money valuable. Every monetary asset, from gold to the dollar, derives its value primarily from social consensus.
