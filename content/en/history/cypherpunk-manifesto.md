On March 9, 1993, Eric Hughes published "A Cypherpunk's Manifesto", a short essay that states the goals of the cypherpunk movement. Its first line is "Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age." Hughes wrote for the cypherpunks, a group that the essay describes as dedicated to building anonymous systems. The text is a statement of principles rather than a technical paper.

The central idea is that privacy is not secrecy. A private matter is one a person does not want the whole world to know, while a secret is one they do not want anybody to know. Hughes argued that every party to a transaction keeps a memory of it and can repeat it, so the only real protection is to reveal as little as possible from the start. He illustrated the point with cash: a person who buys a magazine does not need to tell the shop who they are, and electronic systems should be built with the same restraint.

The manifesto also says that the community has to build the tools itself. It states that cypherpunks write code, that someone has to write the software, and that the group releases its code for free use. It opposes regulation of cryptography and says the cypherpunks are actively working to make networks safer for privacy. The essay describes no protocol, no currency and no system for recording transactions. It sets out values and a method, and leaves the design to whoever writes the software.

The Bitcoin whitepaper does not cite this essay. Its eight references include Wei Dai's b-money and Adam Back's Hashcash, which both came out of the same cypherpunk circle, but the manifesto is not among them. The link to Bitcoin is therefore one of shared goals and community rather than of direct citation. The essay is useful background for the tradition in which many early digital cash proposals were written, and for the view that privacy has to be built into software.
