In March 2013, the Mediterranean island nation of Cyprus was plunged into a severe banking crisis. The Eurozone finance ministers, in coordination with the International Monetary Fund, proposed a bailout package that included an unprecedented measure: a levy on all bank deposits, including those under €100,000 that were supposed to be guaranteed by the state. The proposed "bail-in" would seize up to 9.9% of deposits to recapitalize the failing banks.

![Cyprus bank queue](media/history/cyprus-crisis/cyprus-bank-queue.webp "People queuing outside a bank in Cyprus during the 2013 banking crisis.")

## The Bail-In Proposal

The crisis began when Cypriot banks, heavily exposed to Greek sovereign debt, became insolvent following the Greek debt restructuring. The Eurozone agreed to provide €10 billion in emergency funding, but on the condition that depositors contribute to the cost. The initial proposal included a 6.75% levy on insured deposits under €100,000 and a 9.9% levy on larger deposits.

The announcement caused panic. ATMs were overwhelmed as depositors rushed to withdraw their savings. The government imposed capital controls, limiting daily withdrawals to €300 and banning electronic transfers abroad. For the first time in the Eurozone crisis, ordinary savers were seeing their bank deposits directly confiscated to pay for bank failures.

## Bitcoin's Response

The Cyprus crisis was the first time a major real-world financial event demonstrated Bitcoin's value proposition in action. As news of the bail-in spread, Bitcoin's price surged from approximately $40 to over $260 within weeks. Trading volume on Mt. Gox and other exchanges exploded as people around the world — not just in Cyprus — sought an alternative to the traditional banking system.

The price increase was driven by a combination of factors. Cypriots looking to move wealth out of the banking system discovered Bitcoin as a censorship-resistant alternative. International investors, witnessing the precedent of deposit confiscation, sought assets that could not be seized or frozen by governments. Media coverage introduced Bitcoin to a massive new audience, framing it as "the money the banks can't take."

## The Narrative Takes Hold

The crisis cemented Bitcoin's narrative as "digital gold" and a hedge against banking system risk. The idea that bank deposits could be confiscated — even insured deposits — was deeply unsettling to savers worldwide. Bitcoin, with its decentralized nature and private key ownership, offered a compelling alternative. "Not your keys, not your coins" became more than a slogan — it was now a practical concern.

The Cyprus crisis also highlighted the importance of Bitcoin's fixed supply. While central banks could print money and governments could confiscate deposits, no authority could force Bitcoin to create more coins or seize coins without access to private keys.

## Broader Impact

The Cyprus crisis marked Bitcoin's transition from a niche internet curiosity to a recognized financial alternative. Mainstream media outlets that had previously ignored or dismissed Bitcoin began covering it seriously. The event attracted a wave of new users, developers, and entrepreneurs to the ecosystem. Several prominent Bitcoin companies were founded in the wake of the crisis.

The crisis also started a conversation about the nature of money and banking that continues to this day. For many people, Cyprus was the moment they first asked: "If my bank can take my money, what is money really worth?"
