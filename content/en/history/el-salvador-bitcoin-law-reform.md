On January 29, 2025, the Salvadoran Legislative Assembly reformed the Bitcoin Law that had made Bitcoin legal tender in 2021, enacting changes required by the International Monetary Fund as a condition for a $1.4 billion loan. Bitcoin ceased to be recognized as a currency: its acceptance as payment became voluntary for businesses and could no longer be used to pay taxes. The reform modified six articles and repealed three others, taking effect just days before the IMF's January 31 deadline.

![Salvadoran Legislative Assembly voting on the Bitcoin Law reform in January 2025.](media/history/el-salvador-bitcoin-law-reform/el-salvador-assembly-vote.webp "El Salvador Bitcoin Law reform vote")

The decision marked a dramatic reversal of El Salvador's pioneering experiment. President Nayib Bukele had championed Bitcoin adoption since September 2021, when the country became the first nation to recognize Bitcoin as legal tender. The law mandated that all economic agents accept Bitcoin, established the state-owned Chivo Wallet with a $30 sign-up bonus for every citizen, and exempted Bitcoin from capital gains tax. However, adoption remained low: a study by Yale economist David Argente found that only 20% of Chivo users continued using the wallet after spending the initial bonus, and fewer than 10% used it for Bitcoin transactions specifically.

The IMF agreement, reached in December 2024, required El Salvador to scale back public sector involvement in Bitcoin activities and make private sector acceptance voluntary. In return, the country secured a $1.4 billion Extended Fund Facility aimed at strengthening bank reserves, fiscal sustainability, and growth prospects. The government also committed to reducing the budget deficit by 3.5 percentage points of GDP over three years.

Despite the reform, Bukele's administration maintained its commitment to Bitcoin as a strategic reserve asset. The government continued purchasing one Bitcoin daily — and accelerated purchases after the IMF agreement, acquiring additional coins in what analysts interpreted as a signal of defiance. Tether, the largest stablecoin issuer, announced it was relocating its headquarters to El Salvador shortly after the reform, further underscoring the country's ongoing relationship with the crypto industry. The reform thus represented less an abandonment of Bitcoin and more a tactical compromise: a concession on legal tender status to secure IMF financing, while preserving Bitcoin as a national reserve asset.

International reactions reflected the complexity of the outcome. IMF officials welcomed the reforms as necessary safeguards for financial stability. Bitcoin advocates split between those who viewed the reform as a pragmatic adaptation and those who saw it as a setback for sovereign adoption. For El Salvador, the law change closed the first chapter of the world's largest experiment in national cryptocurrency adoption — but left open the possibility of a second, shaped less by legal tender mandates and more by strategic reserve accumulation.
